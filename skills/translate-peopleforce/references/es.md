# Spanish

Target variety: **neutral Latin American Spanish** — one register for every LatAm market,
not a national variety.

---

## 1. Register — tuteo

*Owner-decided, 2026-08-20, by the LatAm marketing owner. This section replaced a Río de
la Plata voseo register; `translation-corpus/evidence-and-retirement.md` records what went
and why. The reasoning is worth carrying, because it is what makes the rule hold under
pressure: Argentina is the only market that both uses and **accepts** voseo in
professional writing, and even there tuteo is accepted and used professionally. Uruguay
speaks voseo but writes tuteo in formal and professional registers. Everywhere else in
LatAm, voseo is not available at all. The PeopleForce UI is already neutral, so tuteo is
also the choice that keeps product and marketing sounding like one brand.*

**The pronoun is *tú*** — possessive *tu*, object pronoun *te*. A formal legal notice is
the one slot that takes *usted* instead.

**The plural is *ustedes*, never *vosotros*.** The pairing is tú-singular / ustedes-plural,
and it matters for any line addressing a team. A Peninsular plural is a tell rather than a
variant. Measured across 10,035 Spanish turns: `ustedes` 1030 rep / 245 prospect, singular
`usted` effectively absent at 22/14, `vosotros` zero. `usted` is the one address form
prospects use *more* than reps (46 against 34), which is the general pattern — the customer
is the more formal side of the call.

**Provenance, stated because this file distinguishes measured from decided.** The plural
rule is corpus-backed and the `vosotros` zero is a real count. The **singular** rule is
not corpus-derived and must not be argued from that corpus: those calls are
Argentina-weighted sales conversation, which is exactly why they measured voseo. Written
tuteo across LatAm is the owner's market judgement, and it outranks the call data for
published copy.

**Voseo in a human-written Argentine draft is not a defect.** A native writer reaching for
*vos* to sound closer is making a stylistic choice, and a reviewer should leave it alone
rather than flag it as an error. What this skill *produces* is tuteo — every translation,
every review rewrite, every market.

### Conjugation

Present tense and positive imperatives are ordinary tuteo. **Voseo differs from tuteo by
moving the stress to the final syllable, so any second-person present or positive
imperative carrying a final-syllable written accent is voseo and is wrong here** — that
single test catches *podés*, *tenés*, *querés*, *Reservá*, *Elegí*, *Probá*, *Gestioná*
without needing a list. `sos` is the one voseo present form with no accent to catch it;
it is *eres*.

**Tuteo has irregular short imperatives where voseo regularises them**, so this is the
class a voseo habit breaks first. The set is closed: *tener* → **ten**, *venir* → **ven**,
*poner* → **pon**, *hacer* → **haz**, *decir* → **di**, *salir* → **sal**, *ser* → **sé**.

**Tuteo also has stem-changing imperatives that voseo does not**, because voseo takes the
stress off the stem. Where the stem diphthongises, it diphthongises: *probar* → **prueba**,
*contar* → **cuenta**, *empezar* → **empieza**, *volver* → **vuelve**, *mover* → **mueve**,
*elegir* → **elige**, *pedir* → **pide**, *seguir* → **sigue**. De-accenting a voseo form
is not a route to the tuteo one and produces words that do not exist.

**A negative command takes the *tú* subjunctive:** `No manejes`, `No te preocupes`,
`No pierdas`. Under tuteo this is the ordinary form rather than an exception, and an
accented `No perdás` is voseo leakage.

**Established CTA: `Reserva una demo`.** Use it as-is. *(Was `Reservá una demo` — the
published button strings and any live ad copy carrying the old form need updating; see
the handover.)*

**Where the errors will actually come from.** Generic Spanish produces correct tuteo
unprompted, so the live risk is not the model — it is **voseo leaking in from our own
legacy assets and from the sales-call corpus**, both of which are Río de la Plata. Treat
any Spanish lifted from those sources as needing conversion, not reuse.

## 2. Explicitation

English, Polish and Ukrainian all strip words Spanish has to state, so a word-for-word
render comes out grammatical and foreign — the conjugation can be perfect and the line
still read imported. Short copy is where it shows. Two things the source will be missing:
**the article on a mass or abstract noun**, and **the preposition inside a noun stack**
(`datos del empleado`, never `datos empleado`).

**The article rule is about mass and abstract nouns, not about every noun.** A concept or
an uncountable takes the article — `el rendimiento`, `la rotación`, `el onboarding`. A
**plural count** object stays bare, because the article would import definiteness the
English does not have: `No pierdas candidatos`, `Contrata recruiters`. The test is whether
you are naming a quantity of countable things or a concept. §7's own `empresas de cualquier
tamaño` and `tres de cada cuatro empleados` depend on this.

**The imperative's object is conditional.** If the object is not named earlier in the same
line or in the adjacent visual, attach the clitic: `Pruébalo gratis`. If the frame already
names it, the bare imperative is correct: `Prueba gratis`. Decide which case you are in and
hold that choice across the whole set.

**A clitic forces a written accent in tuteo**, because it pushes the stress from the
penult to the antepenult: `pruébalo`, `configúralo`, `descárgalo`, `compártelo`. This is
the exact inverse of voseo, which drops the accent (`probalo`), so an unaccented enclitic
imperative is the single most reliable sign that voseo copy has been half-converted.

**English `the same` splits two ways in Spanish, so the source cannot tell you which.**
Identity — one and the same thing — is `el mismo`; similarity — another one like it — is
`igual`. Decide from what the sentence actually refers to, per sentence, and **never by
find-and-replace**. `mismo` agrees with its noun (`la misma`, `los mismos`).

Identity is the unmarked default and is right more often than not, so **the error to watch
is `el mismo` where the source means *a similar one***. The case that catches people: a
thing each person has their own copy of. `the same first week` is `una primera semana
igual`, because `la misma primera semana` claims one literal week everybody shares.

**Agreement and pronoun marking are governed across a line break too** — the rule is
`SKILL.md` step 3; what is Spanish is that there is no case system, so only two things
reach across a boundary. A participle in a subhead agrees with the headline's noun
(`Una sola plataforma.` → `Diseñada`, not `Diseñado`). An answer takes the form its
question governs, including the `a`: `¿A quién le muestra PeopleForce tus datos
salariales?` is answered `Solo a mí`, not `Solo yo`.

**Gender.** If a line would describe the reader with a participle or an adjective
(`estás registrado`), rewrite it so it does not: `ya estás en la lista`, `tu registro
está confirmado`. Collective and abstract nouns, epicene nouns, second person instead of
third, and infinitives in UI labels all do this cleanly — product copy neutralises
through those devices, not through *desdoblamiento* (`los/las empleados/as`).

## 4. Anglicism policy

### Which terms translate

The glossary rows are the authority. The pattern to internalise: where Polish keeps
the English word, Spanish usually translates it — *panel* for dashboard, *selección*
for screening — and Spanish keeps acronyms, product, brand and customer names in
English per the do-not-translate lists. Where both languages translate (*nómina* /
*płace*, *cumplimiento* / *zgodność*, *flujo de trabajo* / *proces* for workflow),
take each from its own file: `pl.md` §4 states the Polish side.

🚩 **How far anglicisms travel is under owner review and is not settled here.** Two things
are known and both cut against a single rule. Our main ICP is **tech**, where the English
term is usually what the market actually says — so translating it can read stiffer than
leaving it. And how much English is acceptable **varies by industry and by how formal that
industry is**, so the same word is right in one piece and wrong in another. Until the
owner's vocabulary pass lands, follow the glossary row where one exists, and where none
does, take the term the target industry uses and record the choice in the handover rather
than inventing a house form.

Anglicised **verbs** — *setear*, *customizar*, *parametrizar* — were cleared for Río de la
Plata business speech under the old register and are **not yet cleared LatAm-wide**. They
are part of the same pending review. Do not reach for them in neutral copy meanwhile;
*configurar*, *personalizar* and *ajustar* carry the same meaning in every market.

### Four pairs that split by meaning

These are **distinct concepts, not competing translations** — the conditions live on each
glossary row; this is the pattern, and the principle is that **collocation decides, not a
concept split**:

- **recruitment** — `reclutamiento` attracts and sources candidates, `selección` chooses
  among them; both can appear in one sentence, and often should (rows: *recruitment*,
  *screening*).
- **performance** — `Performance` names the product module only; *rendimiento* pairs with
  *gestión* and with measured outputs, *desempeño* pairs with *evaluación* (row:
  *performance*).
- **onboarding** — `onboarding` is the named process and the product capability;
  `incorporación` is flowing prose, the human experience of joining (row: *onboarding*).
- **feedback** — `feedback` leads wherever ordinary marketing prose allows it;
  `retroalimentación` is formal and legal register, where a borrowing would read out of
  place; `comentarios` is literal survey comments, a different concept and never a synonym.
  The split is **register, not sense** (row: *feedback*).

**The gloss move**: on first mention, write the market's natural form and put the other in
brackets after it — *incorporación (onboarding)*, or with the halves swapped where the loan
is the natural form: *feedback (retroalimentación)*. It buys both readings at the cost of
two words, and it works for any pair on this page where one form is the market's word and
the other is the one the reader may already know.

## 5. Punctuation and formatting

Thousands take a **dot** (`1.000`) and decimals a **comma** (`4,6`). Quotes are `“ ”`.
Every question and exclamation **opens** with `¿` or `¡` as well as closing.

**Percent is conditional.** Body copy: `30 %` with the space. A graphic, an ad or any
other fixed canvas: `30%` closed up — what the canonical lifecycle assets do, in every
instance.

**Dashes.** Spanish takes the **raya `—`** for bilateral incisos and for dialogue;
parentheses, a comma or a colon do the same work and are often better in short copy. If
a Polish or Ukrainian source carries the en dash `–`, render it as a raya, parentheses,
a comma or a colon — the en dash does none of those jobs in Spanish.

### Punctuation around a quotation

**The sentence-final period goes after the closing quote, always** — even when the quote
is a complete sentence, and even when the quote ends in `?` or `!`, the carrying sentence
still takes its own period outside: `…sin adivinanzas”.` · `“¿Cuánto cuesta?”.`

**An attribution after the quote joins with a comma, not a dash:** `“Por fin vemos la
semana con anticipación”, dice María.` The raya pair above is for dialogue and incisos,
not for attributing a testimonial.

🚩 Derived from standard orthography (RAE), not yet exercised on a job — confirm against
the first testimonial-heavy piece and record the confirmation in the handover.

## 7. Calques

Source away, read each line as an HR specialist in Mexico City, Bogotá or Buenos Aires
would — someone who buys this software, in any LatAm market rather than one. Then check
the calques below **against the rule, not by ear**: they get past precisely because they
are grammatical Spanish. They are where it happens most often, not the whole set.

**`cada` renders a distributive and nothing else.** Keep it where the source really has
one: governed by a preposition (`para cada miembro`, `en cada etapa`, `de cada cliente`)
and when it opens a clause (`Cada módulo se encarga de un proceso diferente`). One case
takes a plural totality instead — a verbless pair whose other half states a count, where
the point is one-against-many: `Una plataforma, todas las etapas`, not `cada etapa`.

**English `each`/`every` is not always a distributive, and four constructions take Spanish
idiom instead.** A **time span** takes an adjective or a plain preposition
(`informes mensuales`, `16 horas por mes`), not `cada mes` as a default. **Any size** is
`empresas de cualquier tamaño`, not `de cada tamaño`. A **reciprocal** is `entre sí` or
`ayudarse mutuamente`, never `cada uno al otro`. The **exception is a ratio**, where
Spanish idiom carries a `cada` of its own and a plural would be wrong: `tres de cada
cuatro empleados`.

**Noun stacks and bare imperatives** are the other two silent calques — see §2.
