# Spanish

Target variety: **neutral Latin American Spanish** — one register for every LatAm market,
not a national variety.

---

## 1. Register — tuteo

**The pronoun is *tú*** — possessive *tu*, object pronoun *te*. A formal legal notice is
the one slot that takes *usted* instead.

**The plural is *ustedes*, never *vosotros*** — a Peninsular plural is a tell, not a
variant.

**What this skill produces is tuteo** — every translation, every review rewrite, every
market. Voseo in a human-written Argentine draft is a stylistic choice, not a defect —
leave it alone in review.

---

## 2. Explicitation

English, Polish and Ukrainian strip words Spanish has to state, so a word-for-word render
comes out grammatical and foreign — short copy is where it shows. Two things the source
will be missing: **the article on a mass or abstract noun**, and **the preposition inside
a noun stack** (`datos del empleado`, never `datos empleado`).

**The article rule is about mass and abstract nouns, not every noun.** A concept or an
uncountable takes the article (`el rendimiento`); a plural count object stays bare
(`No pierdas candidatos`) — an article there imports definiteness the English does not
have. The test: a concept, or a quantity of countable things?

**The imperative's object is conditional.** Object not named in the line or the adjacent
visual → attach the clitic (`Pruébalo gratis`). Frame already names it → bare imperative
(`Prueba gratis`). Decide which case you are in and hold it across the set.

**A clitic forces a written accent in tuteo** (`pruébalo`).

**English `the same` splits: identity is `el mismo`, similarity is `igual`.** Decide per
sentence from the referent, never by find-and-replace; `mismo` agrees with its noun.
Identity is the default, so the error to watch is `el mismo` where the source means *a
similar one* — a thing each person has their own copy of: `the same first week` is
`una primera semana igual`, since `la misma` would claim one shared week.

**Agreement and pronoun marking are governed across a line break too** (`SKILL.md` step
3). Spanish has no case system, so two things reach across: a subhead participle agrees
with the headline's noun (`Una sola plataforma.` → `Diseñada`), and an answer takes the
form its question governs, including the `a` (`¿A quién…?` → `Solo a mí`, never
`Solo yo`).

**Gender.** If a line would describe the reader with a participle or adjective (`estás
registrado`), rewrite so it does not (`ya estás en la lista`). Collective and epicene
nouns, second person for third, and infinitive UI labels all do this cleanly — never
*desdoblamiento* (`los/las empleados/as`).

## 4. Anglicism policy

The glossary rows are the authority, and each language's policy stands on its own — never
generalised from Polish. The pattern: Spanish usually translates where Polish keeps the
English word, and keeps acronyms, product, brand and customer names per the
do-not-translate lists.

🚩 **How far anglicisms travel is under owner review.** Our main ICP is tech, where the
English term is often what the market says, and tolerance varies by industry — so no
single rule holds yet. Follow the glossary row where one exists; where none does, take
the term the target industry uses and record the choice in the handover.

Anglicised **verbs** (*setear*, *customizar*, *parametrizar*) are not cleared LatAm-wide —
write *configurar*, *personalizar*, *ajustar*.

Pairs that split by meaning or register — *screening*, *performance*, *onboarding*,
*feedback* — are glossary rows; the conditions are in the cells. **The
gloss move**: on first mention, write the market's natural form and bracket the other —
*incorporación (onboarding)* — whichever half is the loan.

## 5. Punctuation and formatting

Thousands take a **dot** (`1.000`), decimals a **comma** (`4,6`). Quotes are `“ ”`. Every
question and exclamation **opens** with `¿` or `¡` as well as closing.

**Percent is conditional.** Body copy: `30 %` with the space. Any fixed canvas — graphic,
ad, chip: `30%` closed up.

**Dashes.** Spanish takes the **raya `—`** for bilateral incisos and dialogue;
parentheses, a comma or a colon often do the job better in short copy. A Polish or
Ukrainian en dash `–` never survives: render it as a raya, parentheses, a comma or a
colon.

### Punctuation around a quotation

**The sentence-final period goes after the closing quote, always** — even when the quote
is a full sentence, and even after `?` or `!`: `…sin adivinanzas”.` ·
`“¿Cuánto cuesta?”.`

**An attribution after the quote joins with a comma, not a dash:** `“…con
anticipación”, dice María.` The raya is for dialogue and incisos, not testimonials.

🚩 From RAE orthography, not yet exercised on a job — confirm on the first
testimonial-heavy piece and record it in the handover.

## 7. Calques

Source away, read each line as an HR specialist in any LatAm market would — someone who
buys this software. Then check the calques below **against the rule, not by ear** — they
pass precisely because they are grammatical. They are where it happens most often, not
the whole set.

**`cada` renders a distributive and nothing else.** Keep it where the source really has
one: governed by a preposition (`en cada etapa`) or opening a clause (`Cada módulo se
encarga de un proceso diferente`). One case takes a plural totality instead — a verbless
pair whose other half states a count: `Una plataforma, todas las etapas`, not
`cada etapa`.

**English `each`/`every` is not always a distributive — four constructions take Spanish
idiom.** A **time span**: an adjective or plain preposition (`informes mensuales`,
`16 horas por mes`), not default `cada mes`. **Any size**: `empresas de cualquier
tamaño`. A **reciprocal**: `entre sí` / `ayudarse mutuamente`, never `cada uno al otro`.
The exception is a **ratio**, where Spanish carries a `cada` of its own and a plural
would be wrong: `tres de cada cuatro empleados`.

**Noun stacks and bare imperatives** are the other two silent calques — see §2.
